\title{In-Band Lattice Zero-Knowledge Proofs for\\ Compressed-Domain H.264 Video Steganography}

\author{Anonymous authors (double-blind review)}

\markboth{Manuscript submitted for review}{Anonymous: In-Band Lattice Zero-Knowledge Proofs for H.264 Video}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Proof-carrying steganography hides a payload in a cover medium together with a
zero-knowledge proof about it, so that a verifier can check a claim from the
medium alone. Existing systems either rely on pairing- or
discrete-logarithm-based SNARKs, which do not survive quantum adversaries,
or deliver the proof beside the medium, where
it is easily stripped or swapped. We present a system that embeds a lattice
zero-knowledge proof directly in the CAVLC residuals of an H.264 Baseline
bitstream and verifies it from the received video, without side files,
metadata boxes or access to the cover. The proof is a Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts
proof of knowledge of an opening of a BDLOP commitment, instantiated at an
estimated 135-bit classical (123-bit quantum) core-SVP level. Its
statement binds a carrier-normalized digest of the video, the
verifier-recomputed carrier positions, a one-use verifier challenge and an
issuer-signed relation registry; carrier normalization breaks the circularity
between a proof and the video that contains it. Two modes are supported: a
7\,589-byte envelope proving possession of a registered credential, and a
14\,309-byte envelope committing to a hidden payload that can later be
disclosed or linked to another commitment. On standard CIF and 4CIF sequences
every completed run verified from the stego video alone and, where tested,
rejected replayed sessions and single-bit tampering; luma PSNR on modified
frames is
40.0--45.4\,dB. Targeted classical steganalysis does not separate cover and
stego, whereas a learned per-frame detector reaches a detection error of 0.40
across clips, which we report as a limitation. We state precisely what the
proof does and does not establish.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Video steganography, H.264/AVC, CAVLC, zero-knowledge proofs, lattice-based
cryptography, post-quantum cryptography, steganalysis.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{C}{ompressed-domain} video steganography modifies the syntax
elements of an encoded stream, such as quantized transform coefficients or
their entropy codes, instead of decoding and re-encoding pixels. It keeps the
stream standard-compliant, avoids re-compression artefacts and, for H.264/AVC,
has a long literature~\cite{ref:liu19review,ref:kunhoth23,ref:mstafa16}.
A recent line of work attaches cryptographic evidence to media: a
zero-knowledge proof that a hidden or withheld value satisfies a relation, so
that a verifier learns that a claim holds without learning the
value~\cite{ref:photoproof,ref:zkimg,ref:veritas,ref:vimz,ref:eva,ref:zkwagon}.
Combining the two gives \emph{proof-carrying steganography}: the medium
carries a hidden envelope that a verifier can check from the medium alone.

Two problems remain open for video. First, the proof systems used in this
setting are pre-quantum: pairing-based SNARKs such as
Groth16~\cite{ref:groth16}, or discrete-logarithm-based folding schemes.
Their proofs are small and easy to hide, but their soundness and the hiding
of the commitments they prove about rest on discrete-logarithm-type
assumptions that a large quantum computer breaks. Media evidence is often
expected to remain checkable for years, so it is worth asking what a
post-quantum alternative costs in a channel whose capacity is measured in
kilobits per hundred frames.

Second, where the proof lives matters. A proof in a side file or a metadata
box (as in content-credential manifests~\cite{ref:c2pa}) is removed by any
re-muxing tool and can be swapped between videos. Moving the proof into the
bitstream creates a circularity: the proof should bind the video, yet it
cannot depend on a hash of a bitstream that contains the proof itself.

This paper addresses both problems for H.264 Baseline (CAVLC) video. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An in-band design.} A lattice zero-knowledge proof travels
  inside H.264 CAVLC residuals and is verified from the stego video alone,
  with no side file, SEI message or cover. A \emph{carrier-normalized} video
  commitment, computed with every carrier coefficient replaced by a canonical
  value and with carrier positions fixed from a placeholder envelope of the
  final length, removes the proof/video circularity
  (Section~\ref{sec:construction}).
\item \textbf{A verifier-built statement.} The Fiat--Shamir statement binds
  the recomputed video commitment, the recomputed carrier positions, a
  one-use verifier challenge, and a relation registry signed by an issuer
  with ML-DSA-65~\cite{ref:fips204}. The verifier never reads a statement
  field from the video, which gives replay protection and anchors trust at
  the verifier.
\item \textbf{Two proof modes and linkage.} A registered mode proves
  possession of an issuer-registered credential (7\,589-byte envelope), and
  an in-band mode commits to a hidden payload of up to 766 bytes (14\,309-byte
  envelope) that can later be disclosed or linked, by an equality proof, to
  another video or to a credential without being revealed.
\item \textbf{A concrete, reproducible instantiation and evaluation.} We give
  a parameter set with core-SVP estimates and known-answer vectors, and we
  evaluate capacity, end-to-end embedding and video-only verification,
  decoded quality, classical and learned steganalysis, the choice of carrier,
  and the cost relative to Groth16, ML-DSA and a lattice zkSNARK
  (Sections~\ref{sec:implementation}--\ref{sec:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}
We are explicit about the limits (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}): soundness is
relaxed, the security argument is in the classical random-oracle model, the
implementation is neither audited nor constant time, and a learned per-frame
detector does separate cover and stego better than chance.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Proof-carrying and provenance media}
PhotoProof~\cite{ref:photoproof} introduced cryptographic image
authentication that survives a set of permissible transformations, using
proof-carrying data over a signed original. ZK-IMG~\cite{ref:zkimg},
VerITAS~\cite{ref:veritas} and VIMz~\cite{ref:vimz} prove image
transformations with SNARKs, lookup arguments or folding schemes, and
Eva~\cite{ref:eva} proves the authenticity of lossily encoded (e.g.\ H.264)
videos with folding-based incrementally verifiable computation and a 448-byte
proof. These systems target provenance of edits from an attested capture;
the proof is an object delivered with the medium, and their assumptions are
pre-quantum. Content credentials~\cite{ref:c2pa} attach signed provenance
manifests as metadata, which survive only as long as the container keeps
them. Closest to us in placement is ZK-WAGON~\cite{ref:zkwagon}, which embeds
a zk-SNARK about an image-generation model into the generated image by
spatial-domain LSB steganography; it is image-domain, embeds a gzip-compressed Halo2 proof of about 100\,kB
in the pixel LSBs, and relies on a pre-quantum SNARK.
Adelsbach and Sadeghi~\cite{ref:adelsbach} proved the presence of a
watermark in zero knowledge, which is the converse problem (a proof
\emph{about} hidden data, kept outside it). To our knowledge no prior system
carries a lattice zero-knowledge proof inside a compressed video bitstream
and verifies it from the stream alone; we make no provenance claim of the
kind PhotoProof or Eva make.

\subsection{Compressed-domain H.264 steganography and steganalysis}
Surveys~\cite{ref:liu19review,ref:kunhoth23,ref:mstafa16} classify H.264
schemes by the syntax they modify: intra-prediction modes, motion vectors,
quantized DCT coefficients and the entropy-coding layer. Coefficient schemes
must control \emph{intra-frame distortion drift}, the propagation of a
modified block into intra-predicted neighbours~\cite{ref:ma10}. Within CAVLC,
the signs of trailing $\pm1$ coefficients (``trailing ones'') can be flipped
without changing the code length~\cite{ref:liao12}; we compare this carrier
with the magnitude-parity carrier we adopt (Section~\ref{sec:carrier}).
Video steganalysis~\cite{ref:bouzegza22} has concentrated on motion-vector
embedding~\cite{ref:wang14mv}; for all-intra streams the natural attacks are
image-steganalysis features applied to decoded frames, such as spatial rich
models with ensemble classifiers~\cite{ref:srm,ref:ensemble} and CNN
detectors~\cite{ref:srnet}, together with targeted histogram attacks on the
modified domain~\cite{ref:westfeld,ref:spa}. Evidence pooled over many
frames of one video is stronger than per-frame evidence~\cite{ref:ker06}, a
point we return to in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Lattice zero-knowledge proofs}
BDLOP commitments~\cite{ref:bdlop} with Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts
openings~\cite{ref:lyu09,ref:lyu12} are the basis of the exact and
linear/product proofs of Attema et al.~\cite{ref:als20}, Esgin et
al.~\cite{ref:ens20} and Lyubashevsky et al.~\cite{ref:lnp22}, implemented
in LaZer~\cite{ref:lazer}; ML-DSA~\cite{ref:fips204,ref:dilithium} uses the
same masking-and-rejection technique for signatures. Succinct lattice
arguments, including LaBRADOR~\cite{ref:labrador},
LatticeFold~\cite{ref:latticefold}, RoKoko~\cite{ref:rokoko} and
SALSAA~\cite{ref:salsaa}, target proof size for large relations; the
versions we could evaluate disable zero knowledge in their reported
configurations, prove knowledge soundness only, or (for folding) do not
provide a standalone zero-knowledge prover. Designated-verifier lattice
zkSNARKs~\cite{ref:isw21,ref:luna} are compact, but the oblivious
LWE-sampling assumption used in the analysis of the standard-model
constructions, including ISW21, is invalidated against quantum
adversaries~\cite{ref:dfs24} (without an attack on the constructions
themselves). We evaluated these candidates as backends against four gates
(zero knowledge, expressibility of the verifier-built statement, runnability
on the target host, and fit in the measured carrier capacity); none passed
all four, which motivates the small, self-contained BDLOP opening proof used
here. The relation we need, knowledge of one commitment opening bound to a
statement, is exactly the one the BDLOP opening proof covers.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{H.264 CAVLC}
In the Baseline profile~\cite{ref:h264,ref:wiegand03}, the residual of each
$4\times4$ block is entropy-coded with CAVLC: a \texttt{coeff\_token} giving
the number of non-zero coefficients and of trailing ones (up to three final
$\pm1$ values in reverse zig-zag order), one raw sign bit per trailing one,
variable-length level codes for the remaining coefficients with an adaptive
suffix length, and run-length codes for the zeros. The context of
\texttt{coeff\_token} depends on the number of non-zero coefficients in
neighbouring blocks, so a change that alters that number propagates to the
coding of later blocks. In intra slices each block is also a prediction
reference for its neighbours, which is the origin of intra drift.

\paragraph{BDLOP commitments}
Let $\Rq=\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N+1)$ with $N$ a power of two and $q\equiv5
\pmod 8$ prime, so that $X^N+1$ splits into two irreducible factors and every
non-zero element of small norm, in particular every difference of two
challenges, is invertible~\cite{ref:ls18}. With public
$B_1=[I_n\,|\,B_1']\in\Rq^{n\times k}$, $b_2=[0^n\,|\,1\,|\,b_2']\in\Rq^{1\times k}$
and $k=n+\lambda+1$, a commitment to $m\in\Rq$ with ternary randomness $r$ is
$t_1=B_1r$, $t_2=\langle b_2,r\rangle+m$. It is computationally hiding under
Module-LWE and binding under Module-SIS~\cite{ref:bdlop,ref:ls15}.

\paragraph{Fiat--Shamir with aborts}
The opening proof is a $\Sigma$-protocol in which the prover masks the
response $z=y+cr$ with a uniform $y$ and aborts unless $z$ lies in a range
that makes its distribution independent of $r$~\cite{ref:lyu09,ref:lyu12}.
It is made non-interactive with the Fiat--Shamir
transform~\cite{ref:fs86}. The original reprogramming argument for this
transform had a gap that was fixed by Barbosa et al.~\cite{ref:barbosa} and
Devevey et al.~\cite{ref:devevey}; quantum-random-oracle results for
Fiat--Shamir~\cite{ref:dfms19,ref:lz19} are generic and not tight for our
setting.

\section{System and threat model}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Roles}
An \emph{issuer} publishes a relation registry signed with ML-DSA-65. Each
registry record pins a proof suite, the hash of the verification key
(including, in registered mode, the holder's credential commitment), the
parameter-set identifier and a policy hash over the codec policy and the
carrier profile; the registry has an epoch. A \emph{holder} owns a payload or
a credential and produces the stego video. A \emph{verifier} issues one-use
session challenges, receives videos and decides. The \emph{adversary}
observes all videos and challenges, replays, edits, re-encodes and re-embeds
videos, runs its own holder instances, and performs steganalysis.

\paragraph{Trust}
The verifier trusts only (i) the issuer public key, (ii) a minimum registry
epoch (its rollback floor), (iii) the parameter set and carrier profile it is
configured with, and (iv) its own session store. Nothing read from the video
is trusted before verification succeeds. The holder and verifier share a
symmetric carrier-ordering (stego) key when undetectability is required; the
key can instead be derived from public configuration
(Section~\ref{sec:orderkey}).

\paragraph{Goals}
(G1) \emph{Soundness bound to video and session}: if the verifier accepts,
then whoever produced the video knew a (relaxed) opening of the bound
commitment, for exactly this carrier-normalized video, carrier layout,
registry entry and session.
(G2) \emph{Zero knowledge}: the video reveals nothing about the payload or the
opening beyond the statement.
(G3) \emph{Integrity}: any change to non-carrier syntax, and any change to
the envelope bytes carried by the carriers, is rejected. (Changes to a few
carrier bits that hold chunk-framing fields are currently not all rejected;
see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.)
(G4) \emph{Replay resistance}: an envelope is accepted at most once, and only
for a challenge the verifier issued and has not consumed.
(G5) \emph{Linkage and disclosure}: a holder can later show that two
commitments hide the same payload, or open a commitment to a chosen party.
(G6) \emph{Undetectability} against an adversary without the stego key,
measured empirically by steganalysis.

\paragraph{Non-goals}
The proof does not establish that the payload is true, that a camera recorded
the video, that an encoder ran correctly, or who the creator is in in-band
mode (anyone holding a session challenge can produce an in-band proof about a
fresh commitment; identity requires registered mode). The channel is fragile
by design: re-encoding or transcoding destroys the envelope, and verification
then fails closed. Robustness is not a goal.

\subsection{Carriers}
\label{sec:carriers}
In every eligible luma $4\times4$ block of an IDR slice, the carrier is the
first AC coefficient in zig-zag order with $|\mathit{level}|\ge4$ that is not a
trailing one. One bit is written into the least-significant bit of its
magnitude by a change of $\pm1$, keeping the sign; the coefficient stays
eligible and, because the selection rule is also required to be
\emph{rank-stable} (no earlier candidate becomes eligible after the flip),
a blind extractor that applies the same rule to the stego stream finds the
same position. A block is used only if its re-encoding with the modified
carrier round-trips through the CAVLC parser of the rewritten slice
(\emph{patchability} check), and at most one coefficient per block is
modified. The
embedder writes the bits by re-encoding the affected blocks and rewriting the
slice payload, which may change its length. An alternative profile uses the
sign of a trailing one as the carrier (Section~\ref{sec:carrier}).

\subsection{Carrier layout and the carrier-normalized commitment}
\label{sec:order}
The stream is divided into segments of $F=100$ frames. The envelope is
framed into chunks of a fixed size (a 23-byte header with magic, version,
index, count, total and chunk sizes and a CRC-32 of the chunk body) and one
chunk is embedded per segment. Within a segment, carrier positions are
ordered by a keyed permutation; the ordering key is derived with HMAC-SHA-256
from the stego key. Let $P$ be the ordered list of global carrier positions
used by all chunks.

The circularity is broken by fixing everything the statement depends on
\emph{before} the proof exists. The embedder first frames a
\emph{placeholder} envelope of exactly the final length; this determines the
chunk sizes and hence $P$. It then computes, for each segment, the SHA-256
digest of the segment bitstream with every carrier coefficient in $P$
replaced by a canonical value (magnitude with its least-significant bit
cleared, sign kept; a sign carrier is set to $+1$), and combines the segment
digests into $D$ with a domain-separated hash. The video commitment is
\[
V=\mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{dom}_V\,\|\,D),
\]
with the domain tag $\mathit{dom}_V$ =
\url{zkstego/video-zk/video-commitment/v1}.
After proving, the embedder checks that the real envelope produces exactly
the placeholder's chunk layout and aborts otherwise. Because $D$ normalizes
exactly the positions that carry the envelope, it is the same before and
after embedding; any change outside the carriers changes $D$, and any change
of an envelope byte invalidates the proof or the statement. The framing
fields themselves are a partial exception (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Statement}
The Fiat--Shamir statement $x$ is the fixed-width (274-byte) encoding
\begin{multline*}
x=\mathit{ver}\,\|\,\mathit{rel}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,\mathit{root}\,\|\,\mathit{epoch}\,\|\,N_s\,\|\\
\mathit{codec}\,\|\,\mathit{profile}\,\|\,|P|\,\|\,\Hash(P)\,\|\,V,
\end{multline*}
with a 16-bit version, 64-bit epoch and carrier count, and 32-byte values
otherwise: relation identifier, parameter-set identifier, registry root,
session challenge, codec-policy identifier, carrier-profile identifier (a hash
of the carrier contract, segment length, chunk size, envelope length and
mode), carrier-position hash and video commitment. The verifier builds $x$
only from its pinned configuration and from values it recomputes from the
received stream.

\subsection{Proof system}
\label{sec:proof}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:proof} gives the opening proof and its verification for
statement $x$ and commitment $t=(t_1,t_2)$; $\Hash$ is SHAKE-256 with domain
separation and length prefixes, and $\SIB$ is the ML-DSA-style sampler that
outputs $c\in\Rq$ with exactly $\kappa$ coefficients in $\{\pm1\}$ and the rest
zero. The masks are derived deterministically from fresh entropy, the
statement, the commitment and the witness (hedged), so a mask is never reused
across statements. Nothing from a rejected attempt leaves the prover. The
equality proof used for linkage runs two copies of the protocol with a common
challenge and adds $u=\langle b_2,y_a-y_b\rangle$ to the hash, which the
verifier recomputes as $\langle b_2,z_a-z_b\rangle-c\,(t_{2,a}-t_{2,b})$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Opening proof (prover) and verification.}
\label{alg:proof}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Prove}$(\pid, x, t, r)$ \Comment{$B=\gamma-\kappa-1$}
\State \textbf{require} $\norm{r}_\infty\le1$ and $B_1r=t_1$
\State $K\gets\mathrm{SHA3\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{entropy}\,\|\,x\,\|\,t\,\|\,r)$
\For{$i=1,\dots,512$}
  \State $y\gets$ uniform in $[-\gamma+1,\gamma]^{kN}$ from $\Hash(\texttt{mask}\,\|\,K\,\|\,i)$
  \State $w\gets B_1y$
  \State $s\gets\Hash(\texttt{fs}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,|x|\,\|\,x\,\|\,t\,\|\,w)$ \Comment{32 bytes}
  \State $c\gets\SIB(s)$;\quad $z\gets y+cr$
  \If{$\norm{z}_\infty\le B$} \Return $\pi=(s,\mathrm{Pack}(z+B))$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\bot$ \Comment{probability $<2^{-178}$}
\Statex
\Statex \textbf{Verify}$(\pid, x, t, \pi)$
\State parse $\pi=(s,z)$; reject unless $|\pi|$ is exact, every packed value
  lies in $[0,2B]$ and padding bits are zero
\State $c\gets\SIB(s)$;\quad $w'\gets B_1z-c\,t_1$
\State \Return $s=\Hash(\texttt{fs}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,|x|\,\|\,x\,\|\,t\,\|\,w')$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

for hiding (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The $\ell_2$ relaxation of the binding
bound exceeds $q$, so binding rests on the $\ell_\infty$ estimate. Under the
estimator's default cost model, every attack on either instance costs at
least $2^{161}$.

\subsection{Envelopes, registry and sessions}
The envelope is
\begin{align*}
E_{\mathrm{in}}  &= \texttt{"ZKLB"}\,\|\,\texttt{0x01}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,N_s\,\|\,t\,\|\,\pi,\\
E_{\mathrm{reg}} &= \texttt{"ZKLR"}\,\|\,\texttt{0x01}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,N_s\,\|\,\pi,
\end{align*}
for the in-band and registered modes. In the in-band mode a fresh
commitment $t$ (6\,720 bytes) to the payload travels in the video; the
payload is encoded injectively into $N$ coefficients and is at most 766
bytes. In the registered mode $t$ is the holder's credential commitment,
whose bytes enter the verification-key hash that the issuer signs into the
registry, so only the credential holder can produce an accepting proof and
the video carries only $\pi$.

The verifier resolves its pins from the registry: it checks the ML-DSA-65
signature against the pinned issuer key, rejects epochs below its floor,
rejects parameter sets with a challenge space below $2^{256}$, and requires
exactly one record matching its parameter set, carrier profile and (in
registered mode) credential. Session challenges are 32-byte random values
stored in a SQLite database with a time-to-live and a binding digest over the
parameter set, relation, registry root and epoch, codec policy and carrier
profile. The verifier reads $N_s$ from the extracted envelope only to look it
up; in a single transaction it checks that the challenge was issued, is
unexpired, unconsumed and correctly bound, verifies the proof under the
statement it built, and marks the challenge consumed only if verification
succeeds.

\subsection{Linkage and disclosure}
\label{sec:linkage}
A holder of two openings can prove that two commitments hide the same
payload with the equality proof (15\,008 bytes), bound to the two
commitments and to a context such as the two statement identifiers. This
links two in-band videos to one payload, or links an in-band video to a
registered credential after the fact, without revealing the payload. A
holder can also disclose the payload to a chosen party without revealing
the randomness: the disclosure is the payload with an equality proof between
the extracted commitment and the deterministic commitment to the payload
under zero randomness, bound to the recipient's statement identifier and
nonce (15\,008 bytes of proof). The recipient learns the payload but cannot
reuse the commitment in new proofs.

\subsection{Carrier ordering key}
\label{sec:orderkey}
With a secret stego key shared by holder and verifier, the carrier layout is
unknown to third parties, which is what steganographic undetectability
requires: without a secret, anyone who knows the scheme can locate the
carriers and test for an envelope~\cite{ref:simmons}. Where undetectability
is not required, the ordering key can be derived from public registered
configuration, $\mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{domain}\,\|\,\pid\,\|\,\mathit{profile})$,
so that anyone with the registry can verify. The key only determines where
bits are found: soundness and zero knowledge of the proof do not depend on
it, because the verifier recomputes $P$ and $V$ and both enter the statement.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Video-level embedding and verification.}
\label{alg:video}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Embed}(cover, payload or credential, pins, $N_s$, key)
\State $E_0\gets$ placeholder envelope of the final length; chunk it
\State $(P, D)\gets$ carrier layout and normalized digest of cover for $E_0$
\State $x\gets\mathrm{Statement}(\text{pins}, N_s, |P|, \Hash(P), V(D))$
\State $E\gets$ envelope with $\pi\gets\textbf{Prove}(\pid, x, t, r)$
\State \textbf{abort} unless $\mathrm{chunks}(E)$ has the layout of $\mathrm{chunks}(E_0)$
\State write $\mathrm{chunks}(E)$ into the carriers $P$ by patching; \Return stego
\Statex
\Statex \textbf{VerifyVideo}(stego, pins, session store, key)
\State $(P', D', E)\gets$ blind extraction (same rule and key); reject on CRC
  or framing error
\State $x'\gets\mathrm{Statement}(\text{pins}, N_s(E), |P'|, \Hash(P'), V(D'))$
\State \Return $\mathrm{ConsumeIf}(N_s(E),\ \textbf{Verify}(\pid, x', t, \pi))$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Security analysis}
\label{sec:security}

We summarize the argument; full statements and proofs follow the published
analyses of BDLOP~\cite{ref:bdlop} and ML-DSA~\cite{ref:dilithium,ref:fips204}
with uniform masking.

\begin{theorem}[informal]
In the classical random-oracle model, the opening proof is complete with
per-attempt acceptance $((2B+1)/2\gamma)^{kN}$; it is honest-verifier zero
knowledge for accepted transcripts; and it is special sound, with extractor
output a relaxed opening $(\bar r,f)$ satisfying $B_1\bar r=f\,t_1$,
$\norm{\bar r}_\infty\le2B$ and $f$ a difference of two challenges, hence
invertible. Two relaxed openings of one commitment to different messages
yield a Module-SIS solution for $B_1$ of $\ell_\infty$ norm at most $8\kappa B$;
hiding reduces to Module-LWE of rank $\lambda$ with $n+1$ samples and ternary
secret and error.
\end{theorem}

Knowledge soundness of the non-interactive proof follows from special
soundness by the forking lemma~\cite{ref:ps00,ref:bn06}, with a non-tight
reduction; the rejection-sampling bias in the random-oracle reprogramming is
handled as in~\cite{ref:barbosa,ref:devevey}. The parameter
identifier, the 274-byte statement and the full commitment are hashed before
the challenge is derived, so a proof verifies only for the statement it was
made for; since the verifier recomputes every statement field, the proof is
bound to the carrier-normalized video, the carrier layout, the registry entry
and the session (G1, and G3 for the envelope bytes), and the atomic consumption of $N_s$ gives G4. The
equality proof yields relaxed openings with a common $f$, from which the two
extracted messages are equal (G5). We call the scheme \emph{plausibly
post-quantum}: the underlying problems have no known efficient quantum
algorithm, but we do not claim a proof in the quantum random-oracle model.
}

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The backend is a self-contained, pure-Python implementation (Python 3.12,
NumPy) of about 1\,700 lines for the ring, sampling, commitment, opening and
equality proofs, payload codec, statement, envelope, linkage and a command-line
interface that separates the issuer, holder and verifier roles. Polynomial
multiplication uses exact big-integer Kronecker substitution; there is no
NTT because $q\equiv5\pmod8$ does not split $X^N+1$ fully. The H.264 side reuses a
Python CAVLC parser and bitstream patcher with a blind carrier
synchronizer, a carrier canonicalizer, the chunk framing, an ML-DSA-65-signed
relation registry (via the \texttt{pqcrypto} bindings) and the SQLite
session store. The test suite covers the ring and samplers, commitment and
proof correctness, the accepted-response uniformity and the acceptance edge,
extraction of relaxed openings from two transcripts, statement substitution,
envelope tampering, registry pinning and rollback, session replay, linkage,
the CLI, and byte-exact known-answer vectors for the parameter set.

\paragraph{Sizes} Commitment 6\,720~B, opening proof 7\,520~B (a 32-byte
challenge seed and $kN$ responses of 18 bits), equality proof 15\,008~B,
envelope header 69~B; hence 7\,589~B (registered) and 14\,309~B (in-band) in
the video, plus 23~B of framing per chunk.

\paragraph{Timing} On the evaluation host (single thread, idle), the median
times over 50 runs are 21\,ms to commit, 120\,ms to prove (95th percentile
302\,ms) and 22\,ms to verify an opening, and 0.97\,s and 46\,ms for the
equality proof. Cryptography is therefore negligible end to end: embedding
and verification of a clip take minutes and are dominated by the Python
CAVLC analysis and patching (Table~\ref{tab:e2e}). A faster verification
path is in progress; \todo{update verification times after the verification
speed-up (roadmap A2)}.

\paragraph{Why not a native encoder path} Embedding inside an encoder would
change the reconstructed reference pixels, and with them all subsequent
intra-predicted values, as a function of the proof bits, so a statement could
not bind the video without a codec-level relation. Post-encoding patching
with a carrier-normalized digest keeps the statement independent of the
proof.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}
All measurements were taken on one Windows~11 host with an Intel Core
i7-12700H (14 cores) and 24\,GB of RAM. Sources are standard Xiph test
sequences: CIF ($352\times288$; 300 frames, except Deadline with 1\,374 and
Football with 260) and 4CIF ($704\times576$; 600 frames). They were encoded
with FFmpeg/libx264 in the Baseline profile (CAVLC), all-intra
($\mathit{GOP}=1$, no B-frames) at a fixed QP of 18, 22 or 28. Two longer
inputs were used for the earliest runs: the first 1\,000 frames of Deadline,
and Coastguard looped ten times to 3\,000 frames (marked $\ast$). Quality is
the FFmpeg luma PSNR of the decoded stego against the decoded cover; the PSNR
of modified frames is computed from their mean MSE, and SSIM is over the
whole video. Unless stated otherwise the registered mode and the magnitude
carrier are used, with 100-frame segments and a chunk size chosen from the
measured capacity of each clip. Experiments ran sequentially, except where
noted in the tables.

\subsection{Capacity}
Table~\ref{tab:capacity} lists stable, patchability-checked carriers. At
QP\,22 CIF clips offer 10.5--49.8\,kbit per 100 frames. Textured content
(Container, Deadline, Coastguard) carries the registered envelope
(about 61\,kbit) in 200 frames, and Container and Deadline carry the in-band
envelope (about 115\,kbit) in 300; flat or
smooth content (Akiyo, Foreman) cannot carry the registered envelope in its
300 frames. Capacity falls steeply with QP (Container: 88.3, 49.8 and
10.0\,kbit at QP 18, 22 and 28), because coarser quantization leaves fewer
coefficients with $|\mathit{level}|\ge4$, and grows with resolution where the
content is textured (City: 23.8\,kbit at CIF, 82.3\,kbit at 4CIF). Capacity
also varies strongly within a clip: one Soccer segment offers only
3.5\,kbit, and Crew offers 11--17\,kbit per segment, so the sweep skipped
both 4CIF clips as not fitting. The capacity scan exposed a defect in the
carrier analysis at non-CIF resolutions, which mapped macroblocks to frames
with the CIF macroblock count and so discarded most of each 4CIF frame; it
failed closed (no wrong data, only lost capacity) and was fixed before the
4CIF runs.

\subsection{End-to-end embedding and video-only verification}
Table~\ref{tab:e2e} reports the end-to-end runs. Every completed run verified
from the stego video alone, and in the runs that exercised them a replay of
the consumed session and a copy with one flipped bit were both rejected; the
payload or credential never appears in the output file. The in-band runs
additionally verified a linkage proof between the commitment extracted from
the video and a separately committed credential, rejected the linkage to a
different credential, and verified a later disclosure of the payload. On
modified frames the luma PSNR is 40.0--45.4\,dB, the worst frame is
32.1--39.3\,dB, and whole-video SSIM is at least 0.9991. The worst frames are
those in which many carriers fall in smooth regions, where intra prediction
spreads a $\pm1$ change to neighbouring blocks. Embedding takes 6--63 minutes
(90 minutes for the concurrently run 3\,000-frame in-band case) and
verification 2--33 minutes per clip in pure Python; the proof itself
takes about 0.1\,s. Pending runs are marked in the table:

\subsection{Steganalysis}
\paragraph{Classical} Targeted tests against the exact cover, on the first
100 frames of each stego video, do not separate cover and stego
(Table~\ref{tab:steg-classical}): the Westfeld--Pfitzmann pairs-of-values
attack on carrier magnitudes~\cite{ref:westfeld} returns an embedding
probability of zero for both cover and stego, the two-sample magnitude
histogram and sign tests give $p\ge0.998$, and SPA on decoded
luma~\cite{ref:spa} changes in the fourth decimal. RS analysis returned no
difference for cover or stego on this content and is not used as evidence.

\input{tables/paper_steg_classical}

\paragraph{Learned} A learned detector is more effective. We extract, per
frame, 340 coefficient-domain features (per-position magnitude histograms,
odd-magnitude rates for $|\mathit{level}|\ge4$, adjacent-position magnitude
co-occurrences and per-position sign shares) and 500 pixel-domain features
from a reduced spatial rich model~\cite{ref:srm} (quantized first- and
second-order residual co-occurrences), and train logistic regression and an
ensemble of shrinkage Fisher linear discriminants on random 30\,\% feature
subspaces (51 members), in the spirit of~\cite{ref:ensemble}. The attacker is
strong: it trains on exact cover/stego pairs from the same encoder settings
and knows which frames carry bits; only modified frames are used. We report
the minimum average error $P_E=\min(P_{\mathrm{FA}}+P_{\mathrm{MD}})/2$. With
leave-one-clip-out evaluation over five CIF clips (1\,300 frame pairs), the
best detector reaches $P_E=0.40$ with coefficient features and $0.45$ with
pixel features; with clips seen in training (a random split of pairs), the
combined features reach $0.36$ (Table~\ref{tab:steg-ml}). The embedding is
thus detectable per frame at a moderate level in its own domain and barely
in the pixel domain. These are per-frame figures; a detector that pools the
evidence of all modified frames of a video~\cite{ref:ker06} would do better
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). A GPU-trained CNN with a fixed SRM
front end (163k parameters, in the style of Yedrouj-Net~\cite{ref:yedroudj18}, 256$\times$256
paired crops, curriculum from an amplified change) does no better: frame
$P_E=0.466$ leave-one-clip-out on 1\,700 frame pairs and $0.46$--$0.47$ on a
random paired split (two seeds), and $0.41$ when its scores are pooled over
each 100-frame segment (Table~\ref{tab:steg-cnn}). The margin is small:
trained and tested on twice the real change, the same network reaches
$P_E=0.18$. The learned evaluation over all clips including 4CIF is

\subsection{Carrier choice}
\label{sec:carrier}
We compared the magnitude carrier with the trailing-one sign carrier used by
earlier CAVLC schemes~\cite{ref:liao12}, through the same pipeline, on four
CIF clips, both at the same number of modified frames as the magnitude
carrier and packed into 100 frames (Table~\ref{tab:carrier}). All twelve runs
verify. Sign carriers are an order of magnitude more plentiful
(468--550\,kbit per 100 frames). At equal frame count they give higher PSNR
on modified frames (43.3--48.8 vs.\ 40.0--45.4\,dB; worst frame 38.5--44.6
vs.\ 32.7--38.8\,dB) but slightly lower whole-video SSIM, and they are much
harder to see in the coefficient domain (leave-one-clip-out $P_E=0.47$ vs.\
$0.39$) yet easier in the pixel domain ($0.40$ vs.\ $0.46$); with combined
features the two are close ($0.40$ vs.\ $0.42$). The CNN separates them
clearly: on sign carriers at the same payload and frame count it reaches
$P_E=0.27$--$0.28$ leave-one-clip-out and $0.14$--$0.17$ on the paired split,
against $0.47$ for the magnitude carrier (Table~\ref{tab:steg-cnn}); sign
carriers should therefore not be presented as secure against learned
steganalysis. Packed into 100 frames,
sign flips are clearly detectable ($P_E=0.31$ with pixel features, and a
per-position sign test flags one clip at $p=0.001$). Sign carriers were
4--8 times slower to verify at equal frame count (2--3 times when packed into
100 frames), because the reduced stable-carrier analysis does not apply to
them; \todo{timing of the optimized sign-carrier analysis
(t1-sign-video-only-v2) once measured on an idle host}. We keep the magnitude carrier as the
default for verification speed and resistance to learned detectors, and
expose the sign carrier as an option when visual quality matters more.

\subsection{Syndrome-trellis coded carriers}
\label{sec:stc}
Because the carrier positions and their number are fixed before the proof
exists, the envelope of a segment can be written as the \emph{syndrome} of
all stable carriers of that segment instead of into a prefix of them. The
contract \texttt{adaptive-stc-magnitude-video-only-v1} codes each segment
with a syndrome-trellis code~\cite{ref:stc} of constraint height 10; the
embedder's Viterbi search chooses the cheapest set of LSB changes, and the
blind extractor reads every carrier and recomputes the syndrome, needing only
the carrier count (unchanged by embedding) and the message length from a
14-byte header coded on the first 112 carriers. The carrier set, its keyed
order and the statement are unchanged apart from the contract identifier,
which the profile identifier binds. Content-based costs computed from
carrier-invariant data added 5--35\,\% more changes than plain change
minimization in simulation, so the default cost is uniform.

On 200-frame cuts of three CIF clips with the registered envelope
(Table~\ref{tab:adaptive-e2e}), STC needs 26--47\,\% fewer changes
(0.27--0.37 instead of 0.50 per envelope bit), gains 1.4--2.4\,dB luma PSNR on
modified frames, and every run verifies from the video alone at the same
cost. Learned detectors do worse against it in every fold and feature set
(Table~\ref{tab:adaptive-steg}): the worst-case $P_E$ rises from 0.41 to 0.43
leave-one-clip-out and from 0.32 to 0.39 on the paired split, while the CNN
stays near chance for both (0.457 vs.\ 0.471). With three clips and a fold
standard error of about 0.03, the evidence is the consistency of the gain,
not its size. The price is fragility: every carrier of a segment enters the
code, so one corrupted carrier anywhere invalidates that segment, which the
verifier already treats as rejection.

\subsection{Comparison with alternative proof systems}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares schemes that could carry the claim.
Groth16 is about fifty times smaller but not post-quantum and needs a

2.4\,MB verifier key, and an analysis based on an assumption invalidated
against quantum adversaries~\cite{ref:dfs24}; its envelope is also larger.
The BDLOP opening proof is the smallest transparent, publicly verifiable,
zero-knowledge option we found that fits the measured capacity.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Relaxed soundness} The extractor yields a relaxed opening
$(\bar r,f)$ rather than a ternary opening, so the payload is bound only
through binding with respect to relaxed openings. This is standard for
Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts proofs, but the statement ``the holder knows the
payload'' must be read in this relaxed sense.

\paragraph{Random-oracle model} The argument is in the classical
random-oracle model with a non-tight forking reduction. We do not claim
security in the quantum random-oracle model; generic results for
Fiat--Shamir~\cite{ref:dfms19,ref:lz19} make a post-quantum interpretation
plausible but do not give concrete bounds for our parameters.

\paragraph{Implementation} The code is a research prototype implemented by
us from the published protocol. It has not been audited and is not constant
time (Python big-integer arithmetic and data-dependent rejection loops);
timing side channels are out of scope.

\paragraph{Framing malleability} The envelope bytes are integrity-protected:
changing any of them makes the proof or the statement fail. The chunk
framing around them is bound as well: the verifier re-chunks the extracted
envelope under the pinned chunk size and rejects unless the extracted chunks
are exactly that encoding. Without this check, an envelope that fits in a
single chunk left the 16-bit chunk-size field free, so carrier bits could be
changed while the same envelope verified.

\paragraph{Stego key versus public order} Undetectability requires a
symmetric key shared with the verifier, which makes verification
designated in practice. The public-order mode makes verification public and
gives up undetectability. The keyed permutation that orders carriers is
derived from an HMAC-SHA-256 seed but uses a chaotic-map shuffle, which is
not a cryptographic pseudorandom permutation; replacing it with a standard
keyed permutation is straightforward and does not affect the proof.

\paragraph{Per-frame versus per-video detection} The learned detector's
per-frame error of 0.40 means that each modified frame leaks a little; a
video carries 100--1\,200 modified frames, and pooling their scores would
likely detect the embedding with high confidence. We have not quantified a
pooled detector. Spreading the envelope over more frames lowers per-frame
density but increases the number of frames that leak; reducing the number
of changes, as the syndrome-trellis coding of Section~\ref{sec:stc} does,
lowers the leakage of every frame and is the more promising direction.

\paragraph{Capacity and codec scope} Only all-intra Baseline streams are
supported, which is a real deployment restriction. A registered envelope
needs 61\,kbit, which smooth content and high QP do not provide within a few
hundred frames. Appendix~\ref{sec:binary-proof} gives a proof that a commitment
under the same parameter set hides a binary string (41\,870 bytes, 2.2\,s to
prove and 72\,ms to verify), built from lifted constant-coefficient relations
and an ALS20-style product step~\cite{ref:als20,ref:lnp22} that works on our
two-slot ring. It is not yet tied to the 24-bit payload encoding, which would
need a bit-decomposition proof, and its multi-round Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts
composition is argued but not written out; range proofs on the payload remain
open.

\paragraph{What is claimed} An accepted video shows that its producer knew a
(relaxed) opening of the bound commitment for this exact carrier-normalized
video, carrier layout, registry entry and session; in registered mode, that
the producer holds the registered credential. It does not show that the
payload is true, that a camera recorded the video, or, in in-band mode, who
produced it. The session challenge is not secret, so it should be delivered
over an authenticated channel if the holder's identity matters.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Internal validity} The protocol, parser and patcher were
implemented by the authors; correctness is supported by unit and negative
tests, known-answer vectors and the end-to-end verification itself, but not
by an independent implementation. Quality is measured against the decoded
cover, not the raw source, which isolates the embedding from the encoder.
Timings come from a single host; two long runs were executed concurrently
and are marked. The density and rule comparisons are simulations that omit
the patchability filter.

\paragraph{Construct validity} $P_E$ is a per-frame measure on modified
frames only, which favours the attacker within a frame but ignores pooling
across frames. PSNR on modified frames depends on how many frames an
envelope touches, which in turn depends on the chosen chunk size. Capacity
for partly scanned clips is extrapolated from the scanned segments.

\paragraph{External validity} The evaluation uses a small set of standard
CIF and 4CIF sequences, one encoder (x264), the Baseline profile, all-intra
coding and fixed QP. Results may differ for camera footage, other encoders,
rate control, P-slices or higher resolutions. The detectors are
feature-based; a stronger learned detector may lower $P_E$.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity} Learned results are averaged over five
held-out clips with no confidence intervals; we report the range over folds
instead. With few clips, differences of a few hundredths in $P_E$ between
configurations should not be over-interpreted.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A post-quantum zero-knowledge proof can travel inside an H.264 CAVLC
bitstream, bound to that bitstream through a carrier-normalized commitment
and to a verifier session, and be verified from the video alone. With a
7.6\,kB registered envelope and a 14.3\,kB in-band envelope, textured CIF
and 4CIF content carries the proof in one to three hundred frames at
40--45\,dB on modified frames, and classical targeted steganalysis does not
detect it. A learned per-frame detector does better than chance, and the
security argument rests on relaxed soundness in the classical random-oracle
model. The next steps are cost-aware carrier selection against learned and
pooled detectors, tying the binary proof to the payload encoding, support
for P-slices and higher resolutions, and an independent review of the
cryptographic argument.
